The best AI agents for startups from pre-revenue to Series A: coding, research, sales, support and finance picks plus Viktor, the AI employee.

## Key Takeaways

By Kris Newlin. Updated October 27, 2026.

Which AI agents should a startup use? Start with Viktor, an [AI employee](https://viktor.com/ai-employee) who works in Slack or Microsoft Teams across 9,800+ tools. Then add one agent per bottleneck: Claude Code or Cursor and Perplexity before revenue, Clay or Apollo and Intercom Fin at seed, Ramp and a data agent at Series A, and a browser agent such as ChatGPT agent or Browser Use for web tasks with no API.

- **Stage beats category:** A three-person pre-revenue team and a thirty-person Series A team have different bottlenecks, budgets and owners. Pick for the stage you are in, not the one in the pitch deck.
- **One AI employee, a few specialist agents:** Viktor sits at #1 because he covers the cross-tool work no single agent owns. Specialist agents earn their place only when one job is big enough to justify another tool.
- **Buy before you build:** Before product-market fit, every engineering hour spent building internal agents is an hour not spent on the product. Build only when the workflow is your differentiator.
- **Budget in hours saved, not seats:** Most agents now charge by usage (credits, actions, resolutions). Compare the monthly cost with the founder or hire hours the agent actually removes.

## The short answer

If you only read one section, read this. Here is what we would adopt at each stage, in order:

1. **Every stage: Viktor.** He takes recurring work across your tools (metrics, CRM hygiene, follow-ups, reports, small builds) from a plain-language request in Slack or Teams. Free to start with up to $100 in credits.
2. **Pre-revenue (1-5 people):** Claude Code or Cursor for shipping the product, Replit Agent if no one on the team writes code, and Perplexity or ChatGPT deep research for market and customer research.
3. **Seed (5-15 people):** Clay or Apollo for outbound once you know your ideal customer, and Intercom Fin for support once ticket volume starts to eat founder time.
4. **Series A (15-50 people):** Ramp for spend and expenses, Julius AI or a warehouse-connected data agent for analysis, and a workflow builder such as n8n or Zapier for high-volume fixed pipelines.
5. **Web tasks with no API (any stage):** Viktor with his own login to the tool, ChatGPT agent if you already pay for ChatGPT, or Browser Use and Skyvern if an engineer wants open source.

## Why stage matters more than agent type

Most “best AI agents” lists sort tools by type: coding, sales, support, research. That tells you what exists, which our guide to [AI agents for business](https://viktor.com/blog/ai-agents-for-business) already covers. It does not tell you what to adopt next, and for a startup that is the only question that matters.

Three things change as a company grows, and each one changes the right answer:

- **The bottleneck moves.** Before revenue it is building the product and finding customers. At seed it is repeatable pipeline and support. At Series A it is coordination: reporting, finance, data and handoffs.
- **The owner changes.** At three people the founder checks every tool. At thirty a functional lead owns each workflow, and an agent nobody owns stops being used.
- **The cost of a mistake rises.** A wrong email to ten prospects is an annoyance before revenue. A wrong number in a board report at Series A is a real problem, so review steps and permissions matter more than raw capability.

That is why copying another stage’s stack wastes money: the tools are fine, the stage is wrong.

## How we picked

We shortlisted agents that founders actually ask about and judged each one on five criteria:

1. **Fit for the stage’s bottleneck.** Does it remove the work that is slowing this stage down?
2. **Time to first useful result.** Hours, not weeks. Startups cannot run a quarter-long rollout.
3. **Who has to own it.** Can a non-technical founder run it, or does it need an engineer to maintain?
4. **Pricing model.** Seats, credits, actions or per-resolution fees, taken from each vendor’s pricing page in October 2026 (Viktor’s from [viktor.com/pricing](https://viktor.com/pricing)).
5. **Control near real data.** Approval steps, permissions and an audit trail before it changes anything customers or investors see.

## Pre-revenue (1-5 people)

At this stage you have one job: build something people want and prove it. Agents should save founder hours on building and learning, nothing else. For the founder-level playbook, see [AI for startup founders](https://viktor.com/blog/ai-for-startup-founders).

### Coding agents

A coding agent is the highest-leverage purchase for a technical team of one to five. It reads your codebase, writes and edits code across files, runs tests and opens pull requests.

- **Claude Code (Anthropic):** works in the terminal and IDE, strong on multi-file changes and long tasks. Included with Claude Pro at $20 a month billed monthly ($17 a month billed annually) and Max from $100 a month (checked October 2026). Wrong for teams that want a visual editor first.
- **Cursor:** an AI-first code editor with agent mode for multi-step changes. Free tier, paid individual plans from $8 a month and Teams from $30 per user a month (checked October 2026). Wrong for developers who will not switch editors.
- **GitHub Copilot:** the default if your code already lives on GitHub, with agent mode and pull-request work. Free plan, Pro at $10 a month and Pro+ at $39 a month (checked October 2026). Wrong for teams that need deep autonomy on large refactors.
- **Replit Agent:** builds and hosts a working app from a prompt, which suits non-technical founders testing an idea. Core from $20 a month, or $18 billed annually (checked October 2026). Wrong for production systems you plan to scale.

Our view on the fully autonomous end of this spectrum is in [Viktor vs Devin vs Manus](https://viktor.com/blog/viktor-vs-devin-vs-manus). Keep the coding agent in your dev workflow and Viktor in Slack: they cover different work and coexist well.

### Research agents

Research agents read dozens of sources and return a cited report on competitors, market size or a new market’s rules.

- **Perplexity:** fast, cited answers and deeper research reports, good for daily market questions. Free tier and paid Pro plans on the vendor’s pricing page.
- **ChatGPT deep research and Claude research:** longer, multi-step research reports from the assistants many founders already pay for. Available on paid plans; Claude Pro is $20 a month billed monthly (checked October 2026).

Wrong for anything that has to land in another system: turning findings into a CRM list or a tracked task is still manual, which is where Viktor picks up.

## Seed (5-15 people)

With funding and the first repeatable customers, the bottleneck moves to pipeline and support. Each agent now needs an owner and a clear metric.

### Sales and outreach agents

Sales agents research accounts, enrich contacts, write personalised outreach and keep sequences running.

- **Clay:** data enrichment from many providers plus Claygent, its research agent, for building targeted lists. Free plan with 500 actions a month; Launch from $167 a month (checked October 2026). Wrong for teams without a defined ideal customer profile, because enrichment at scale amplifies bad targeting.
- **Apollo:** a contact database, sequences and AI-written emails in one tool, a common first sales stack at seed. Free plan, with paid plans priced per user on Apollo’s pricing page. Wrong for teams selling into niches its database covers thinly.

Viktor works between these tools: he keeps HubSpot or your CRM clean after the sequences run, flags stale deals and prepares account briefs before calls. See [AI for sales](https://viktor.com/blog/ai-for-sales) for more patterns.

### Support agents

A support agent answers common questions from your help docs and hands harder cases to a human.

- **Intercom Fin:** resolves conversations from your knowledge base and can sit on top of an existing helpdesk. From $0.99 per Fin outcome, with Intercom seats from $29 a month ($19 for new customers on annual plans) (checked October 2026). Wrong for very low volume, where a shared inbox is cheaper.

Turn a support agent on only when you have written help content worth answering from. For the wider support playbook, read [AI for customer support](https://viktor.com/blog/ai-for-customer-support).

## Series A (15-50 people)

At Series A the problem is coordinating the work, not doing it. Reporting, finance, data requests and handoffs multiply, and many startups make their first ops hire.

### Ops, finance and data agents

- **Ramp:** corporate cards, expense management, bill pay and accounting automation with AI that codes transactions and collects receipts. Free plan at $0 per user (checked October 2026), with paid tiers for more controls. Wrong for companies outside the regions Ramp serves.
- **Julius AI and warehouse data agents:** ask questions of spreadsheets or your data warehouse in plain language and get charts back. Paid plans on each vendor’s pricing page. Wrong for questions where nobody trusts the underlying data yet.
- **n8n or Zapier for fixed pipelines:** when a workflow is identical every time (a form fills, a record syncs, a notification fires), a builder runs it cheaply. n8n Cloud Starter is $20 a month billed annually (checked October 2026). ## Browser and computer-use agents: Operator alternatives for startups

Some startup work lives in web apps with no usable API: supplier portals, tax and licensing forms, legacy CRMs, competitor sites. Browser and computer-use agents open a browser, read the screen and click and type the way a person would. This is the category most founders mean when they search for OpenAI Operator alternatives.

- **Viktor:** he works in tools without an API by signing in with his own login, the way a new hire would, on a computer in the cloud. You choose the permissions, he holds risky actions as drafts for your yes in Slack or Teams, and you can revoke the login in one click. See how [Viktor works without an API](https://viktor.com/no-api).
- **OpenAI ChatGPT agent (formerly Operator):** Operator launched in January 2025 for ChatGPT Pro users and was folded into ChatGPT agent mode later that year. Agent mode now runs on paid ChatGPT plans, and Pro is sold in usage tiers (checked October 2026 on chatgpt.com/pricing). The top Pro tier is worth it only if a founder runs long agent tasks most days. For occasional web tasks, a lower paid plan or one of the options below is enough.
- **Claude computer use (Anthropic):** Claude computer-use is an API tool: your code sends Claude a screenshot, Claude replies with the next mouse or keyboard action, your code performs it in a sandboxed virtual machine or container and sends back the new screenshot, in a loop until the task is done. You pay standard Claude API token prices, and every screenshot adds tokens, so long tasks cost more than short ones. It is the main developer option from OpenAI’s biggest rival in the agent space, alongside Google’s Gemini computer-use work and Amazon’s Nova Act. Wrong for non-technical teams: there is no finished app, you build the loop and the sandbox yourself.
- **Browser Use:** the headline open-source alternative. The Python library is free and MIT-licensed, works with models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek and others, and runs locally, headless or in Docker. Browser Use Cloud adds hosted browsers from $0.02 an hour, model cost plus a 20% fee and $5 per GB of residential proxy traffic (checked October 2026).
- **Skyvern:** open source under AGPL-3.0, built for repeatable form-filling and portal workflows, with stored credentials, 2FA support and a CAPTCHA solver in its cloud plans. Cloud Hobby is $29 a month for 30,000 credits and Pro $149 a month (checked October 2026). It is also the usual answer to “what are some alternatives to Skyvern”: Browser Use for general tasks, Stagehand for developers.
- **Stagehand (Browserbase):** an MIT-licensed SDK for developers who want to mix code with AI steps, so stable parts of a flow run as plain code and only the fuzzy parts use a model.

### What self-hosting a browser agent really costs

The library is free. The model is not. Browser Use publishes a benchmark of cost and quality per model on its pricing page: in October 2026, a run cost from about $0.06 per task on a small fast model to almost $9 per task on the strongest frontier model, with quality scores from about 12 to 81 out of 100. For the same 100 tasks a month, that is roughly $6 to $15 in tokens on a cheap model and several hundred dollars on a frontier one, plus a server and an engineer’s time to keep scripts and logins working.

Running it yourself is a short setup for an engineer: install the Python library, add Playwright and a Chromium browser, set the model API key, and run the agent locally to watch it work. For scheduled jobs, switch to headless mode and package it in Docker on a small server, or keep everything local-first on a laptop when the data must not leave your machine. Skyvern ships a pip quickstart and a Docker Compose file with its own Postgres database for the same purpose.

Cheaper and open-weight models (DeepSeek, Llama, Gemini Flash, which tutorials still recommend in their older DeepSeek R1, Llama 3.3 and Gemini 2.0 Flash versions) cut the token bill but fail more often on long, visual tasks, so vision-capable models matter for anything with complex pages. For most startups under 15 people, maintenance hours cost more than tokens: if nobody owns the setup, a subscription or Viktor is cheaper.

### How reliable are browser agents?

Vendors quote high scores on the WebVoyager benchmark: in early 2025, OpenAI reported 87% for the model behind Operator, Browser Use 89.1% and Skyvern 85.85%. These are self-reported, on public websites and short tasks. Skyvern’s own repository now reports 64.4% on the harder WebBench, and Browser Use’s 2026 benchmark puts most models well below 80 out of 100. In practice, expect retries, CAPTCHAs and session timeouts, and keep a person on anything that submits payments or customer data.

### The developer stack for testing browser agents

If an engineer builds browser agents in-house, the usual stack has three layers. Orchestration: LangChain or CrewAI in Python, or the Vercel AI SDK in TypeScript, to chain model calls, tools and retries. Browser control: Playwright underneath, with Browser Use or Stagehand on top. Sandboxes: hosted browser infrastructure such as Steel (open source) or Browserbase, so agents run in isolated sessions with test accounts, recordings and logs instead of a founder’s real logins. Run every new agent against test accounts for a week before it touches production data.

## How to choose at your stage

Use these five steps before you add any agent:

1. **Name the bottleneck.** Write down the one task that costs the most founder or team hours this month. If you cannot name it, you do not need a new agent yet.
2. **Check if Viktor already covers it.** If the work spans several tools and changes week to week, give it to Viktor first. Add a specialist only when one job is big enough to justify its own tool.
3. **Decide build or buy.** Buy for anything that is not your product. Build only when the workflow is a core part of what you sell, and you have engineers to maintain it. Our guide on whether to [build an AI agent or hire an AI employee](https://viktor.com/blog/build-ai-agent-or-hire-ai-employee) goes deeper.
4. **Run a two-week pilot with a number.** Measure hours saved, tickets resolved or meetings booked against a baseline. Keep the agent only if the number moves.
5. **Set permissions and an owner.** Give each agent the least access it needs, require approval for anything customer-facing or financial, and name the person who checks its work.

### Credentials, OAuth and the tasks you should never hand an agent

Prefer OAuth with narrow scopes over shared passwords, give any agent that logs in its own account rather than a founder’s, store logins in a password manager (Skyvern, for example, integrates with 1Password), and revoke access when a pilot ends. Viktor connects most tools through one-click OAuth and asks before anything hard to undo. Do not let any agent act alone on payments and refunds, raw customer PII, production credentials or infrastructure, or legal and HR decisions. Draft yes, execute with a human approval. Our [security checklist for AI tool access](https://viktor.com/blog/security-checklist-for-ai-tool-access) covers the details.

### A 30-day rollout plan for a team of five

- **Week 1:** connect Viktor to Slack and your core tools, and hand him one recurring report.
- **Week 2:** add the one specialist agent your bottleneck needs (usually coding or research) and record a baseline.
- **Week 3:** move a second recurring workflow to Viktor and test one browser task in a sandbox if you have a no-API tool.
- **Week 4:** compare hours saved with the baseline, cancel what did not move the number, and write down the approval rules.

Rule of thumb: one or two agents before revenue, three or four at seed, and at Series A as many as you have owners for.

## FAQ

### What is the best AI agent for a startup?

It depends on your stage. Pre-revenue teams get the most from a coding agent such as Claude Code or Cursor plus a research agent such as Perplexity. Seed teams add sales and support agents like Clay, Apollo or Intercom Fin. Series A teams add finance and data agents like Ramp and Julius AI. At every stage, Viktor is our #1 pick as the AI employee who handles cross-tool work in Slack and Teams that no specialist agent owns.

### Should a startup build or buy AI agents?

Buy, in almost every case. Before product-market fit, engineering time belongs on the product, and off-the-shelf agents are faster to adopt and cheaper to maintain. Build only when the agent is part of what you sell or the workflow is a real competitive advantage, and you have engineers who can own it after launch.

### How is an AI employee different from an AI agent?

An AI agent is usually built for one job, such as writing code, answering tickets or enriching leads. An AI employee like Viktor takes on many kinds of work across your tools from plain-language requests in Slack or Teams, remembers how your team works, and asks for approval before he acts. Startups typically run one AI employee alongside a few specialist agents.
